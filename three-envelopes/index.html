Three envelopes, 26 years: what investing really pays you for

The same money went into the US, Malaysian and Indonesian stock markets in 2000. Where it ended up explains why most small investors lose, and what you can do differently.

Reka Amorra · 25 October 2026 · 15 min read
The short version
  • US$10,000 put into US stocks at the end of 1999 is worth roughly US$86,000 today. The same money in Indonesia is about US$61,000, and in Malaysia about US$45,000.
  • In January this year, the Indonesian envelope was worth about US$107,000, more than the US one. Then the IHSG fell around 40% from its record.
  • Missing just the 10 best days in 20 years of US stocks cut the end result by more than half.
  • High fees can quietly take about a third of your gains over 20 years.
  • Wealth comes from owning businesses that keep getting more productive, bought at sensible prices and held for a long time. Most people lose because they do the opposite.

Where does the money from investing actually come from? I have been asking myself this, where I am for a running event, I was here when the IHSG dropped more than 11% in one day.

Everyone around me was talking about stocks. Many of them only opened their accounts this year. By August, Indonesia had added almost 10 million new capital market investors in 2026 alone. A lot of them bought when the index was above 9,000. Today it is around 5,400.

So I went back to basics and did some homework. To make it simple, I imagined three envelopes. Each one had the same money, around US$10,000, RM38,000 or Rp70 million in late 1999. One went into America's 500 biggest companies. One went into Bursa Malaysia. One went into the Jakarta stock exchange. Nobody touched them for 26 years. This week, I opened them.

Before I show you the results, I need to explain a few things I learned along the way. They changed how I think about money.

01Where money really comes from

Money itself is not wealth. It is a promise. A ringgit or a rupiah is only worth something because we all agree it can be swapped for someone's work, food, petrol or rent.

Much of this section comes from Meng Yan, founder of the Chinese investing platform Youzhiyouxing. His explanation is the clearest I have found, so I want to credit him properly.

His point is simple enough. Real wealth is the stuff people produce. Over time, machines, factories, software and better ways of working let each person produce much more in an hour than before. That is where long-term growth comes from. It does not appear from nowhere.

Meanwhile, banks and governments create new money faster than the economy produces new goods. So each unit of money slowly buys less. You see it at the pasar every year.

What the data says

At the end of 1999, one US dollar cost about Rp7,100. In October 2026 it costs about Rp18,000. Measured in dollars, the rupiah lost around 60% of its value. The ringgit was fixed at RM3.80 back then and is around RM4.09 today, a loss of about 7%.

This is why keeping all your savings in cash feels safe but is not. It shrinks quietly.

A share in a company is different. It is a small piece of ownership in a business that sells things, earns profit and, if it is a good business, grows. When a company becomes more productive, the owners get that gain. Over long periods and across most countries, owning a broad basket of companies has beaten gold, bonds and cash.

So the honest answer to my question is this. Investing pays you for owning a slice of other people's productive work, and for being patient while that work compounds.

02Why most small investors still lose

If owning companies works so well over time, why do so many retail investors lose money? The numbers are not kind.

India's market regulator SEBI studied its derivatives market and found that 91% of individual traders lost money in the year to March 2025. A well-known study of day traders in Taiwan by Barber, Lee, Liu and Odean found that fewer than 1% made reliable profits year after year.

Malaysia and Indonesia do not publish the same kind of study. But from what I read, people lose for three reasons, wherever they are.

Leak 1

Paying too many helpers

Sales charges, management fees, advisers and trading costs. Each one looks small. Over 20 years they add up to a big bite.

Leak 2

Following the crowd

Buying when everyone is excited and the price is already high, then selling in fear when it falls.

Leak 3

Jumping in and out

Trying to time the market. Missing a handful of big up days can wipe out most of your return.

Leak 1: The helpers

Warren Buffett tells a story in his 2005 letter to shareholders. He imagines a rich family, the Gotrocks, who own every company in America. They get all the profits for free. Then "helpers" arrive. Brokers, managers and consultants each promise to help the family do better than its relatives. Each takes a fee. The family ends up with less, not more.

In Malaysia, many unit trusts charge an upfront sales charge of up to 5.5%, plus a yearly management fee. Low-cost index funds and ETFs often charge a fraction of that.

High fees took about RM52,000 from the same savings plan
Illustration, not data. RM500 a month for 20 years at an assumed 8% gross return a year. High cost assumes a 5.5% sales charge on each top-up and a 1.5% yearly fee. My own calculation.

In this example the high-cost plan lost about a third of all the gains. Nobody sent a bill. It just never showed up in the account.

Leak 2: The crowd

In the short run, prices are not set by careful people. They are set by whoever is most excited to buy at that moment, or most scared to sell. When everyone is excited, the price you pay already includes years of hope.

Malaysians remember the glove rally. During the pandemic, Top Glove shares traded above RM8 in September 2020, after a bonus issue. Last week they were around RM0.88. The company did not disappear. People simply paid a price that assumed the pandemic boom would last forever.

Indonesians have their own version. GoTo listed in April 2022 at Rp338 a share, with a lot of excitement about Indonesian tech. In October 2026 it trades around Rp24.

Then there is saham gorengan in Indonesia, or goreng counters in Malaysia. These are small, thinly traded stocks that get pushed up fast, often with WhatsApp and Telegram tips, then dumped on latecomers.

Trap

The IHSG hit a record of 9,032 on 14 January 2026. On 16 October it fell 11.57% in a single day to 5,185, after MSCI proposed moving Indonesia out of its emerging markets index over concerns about free float, ownership transparency and coordinated trading. Even big banks like BCA, BRI and Mandiri hit their daily lower limit (ARB). Many first-time investors bought in that January excitement.

Leak 3: Jumping in and out

The natural reaction to a crash is to sell and wait until things "calm down". The problem is that the best days usually come right after the worst ones, while everyone is still scared.

Missing the 10 best days cut the result by more than half
US$10,000 in the SPDR S&P 500 ETF (SPY), dividends reinvested, October 2006 to October 2026. Source: The Motley Fool.

Forty days out of about 5,000 trading days. Less than 1% of the time. Miss those, and twenty years of investing ends in a loss.

Lesson

What works is quite plain. Good businesses, bought when they are not overpriced, held long enough for the compounding to happen. Most people lose because they buy whatever is hot, pay too much in fees, and leave at the worst moment.

03Three markets, three personalities

The same rules apply everywhere. But each market makes its money in a different way, and each has its own way of trapping people.

United States

The global engine

The S&P 500 holds companies like Apple, Microsoft, Nvidia and Alphabet that sell to the whole world. The index drops weak companies and adds rising ones, so it cleans itself.

Index ETFs cost as little as 0.03% a year.

Trap: meme stocks, short-dated options, panic selling.

Malaysia

The dividend cow

Banks, utilities, plantations and infrastructure. Steady businesses that pay out a big share of profit as dividends. The KLCI price has roughly doubled since 1999, but dividends more than doubled it again.

Trap: penny stocks, warrants, buying the hype at the top.

Indonesia

The young economy

Around 280 million people with a young median age, more of them opening bank accounts and paying digitally every year. The big banks grew with that.

Trap: saham gorengan, margin trading, governance worries.

Malaysia: a good company is not always a good buy

Inari Amertron shows both sides of Bursa. It is a real productivity story. It moved into testing and packaging radio-frequency chips used in smartphones, and analysts called it a close proxy for 5G. The shares hit a record RM4.18 in November 2021.

By May 2026, the same shares were around RM1.98. The company still works. But anyone who bought at the 2021 excitement paid for growth years in advance, and is still waiting to get back to break-even.

The quiet strength of Bursa is dividends. Many big Malaysian companies pay out 3% to 4% a year. If you only look at the KLCI price, it looks like nothing happened for a decade. Once you add dividends back, the picture is very different.

Indonesia: growth is real, so is the risk

Indonesia's growth story is real. Millions of people moving from cash to bank accounts and mobile payments made banks like Bank Central Asia (BCA) some of the best businesses in the region for many years.

But growth does not protect you from price. BCA opened at Rp5,300 on 21 October 2026, well below the Rp6,400 it traded at in mid-September, even though the bank itself did not change much in a month.

The MSCI issue is also a reminder that market quality matters. MSCI is taking feedback until April 2027 and will decide by June 2027. Free float, who really owns what, and how prices are set all affect what local investors get in the end.

If you are not comfortable picking single stocks, the same idea works with a basket. In the US that is the S&P 500. In Malaysia it could be the big banks and utilities together, or a KLCI tracker. In Indonesia it could be the big four banks together, or an index fund tracking large companies. These are examples to help you understand the idea, not recommendations.

Three things stood out for me.

First, every envelope grew a lot. Even Malaysia, the "boring" one, turned RM38,000 into about RM183,000. That is around 6% a year, mostly thanks to dividends. Cash in a drawer would have lost value instead.

Second, currency matters as much as the market. Indonesian companies grew faster than American ones in local terms. But an Indonesian who held only rupiah assets would have ended up with less purchasing power in dollar terms than someone who also held US stocks.

Third, when you open the envelope matters. In January, Indonesia was the winner. Nine months later it is well behind. Nothing about the long-term growth of Indonesia changed in those nine months. Only the mood did.

Watch

These are estimates, not exact total return figures. I used average dividend yields, not actual yearly payouts, and ignored taxes, fees and dividend withholding. Foreign investors in US stocks usually pay withholding tax on dividends. The ranking is solid, but treat each number as a rough guide.

05What I am taking from this

The following are my own analysis, not findings from the sources.

I am not a financial adviser. These are the changes I think make sense for ordinary families in Malaysia and Indonesia, based on what I learned.

1

Build your safety cushion first

Keep three to six months of expenses in cash or savings before you buy any stock. Then a crash does not force you to sell.

Measure: months of expenses you can cover
2

Find out what you pay

Look at your unit trust or fund statement this week. Write down the sales charge and the yearly fee. Anything above 1% a year needs a good reason.

Measure: total yearly cost in %
3

Invest on a fixed date every month

A fixed amount on a fixed date, every month. You buy more when prices are low and less when they are high, without having to guess.

Measure: months in a row you invested
4

Do not keep everything in one currency

Hold some home-market shares for dividends and local spending, and some global shares for growth and currency protection. The split depends on your situation.

Measure: share of savings outside your home currency
5

Only buy what you can explain

If you cannot say in one sentence how a company makes money, skip it. That rule alone removes most gorengan, penny stocks and Telegram tips.

Measure: stocks you hold that you can explain
6

Plan for a 40% fall before it happens

Decide now what you will do when the market drops hard. Write it down. Indonesia just showed a 40% drop can happen within one year.

Measure: a written plan you have actually read
Self-check
  1. How many months of expenses could I cover if my income stopped?
  2. Do I know exactly how much I pay in fees each year?
  3. How much of my savings is in a single currency?
  4. Did I buy anything this year mainly because other people were buying it?
  5. If my investments fell 40% next month, would I need to sell?

When I started this homework, I wanted to know where investment money comes from. What I found is quite ordinary. It comes from people working, companies getting better at what they do, and owners being patient enough to stay.

The envelopes did not need anyone to be clever. They only needed nobody to touch them. That is the part most of us find hardest. Maybe that is a good thing to think about on a slow Sunday.
