Three democracies, three ways to fail.

Why Malaysia had four prime ministers in four years, why America's checks are breaking, why Indonesia erupts in protests — and which of the three designs actually handles failure best.

Reka Amorra · 5 November 2026 · ~18 min read

The short version

  • Malaysia's design is the most resilient. A Westminster parliament can remove a failing government in weeks through a confidence vote. Four prime ministers in four years was ugly, but the state kept working and the anti-hopping law patched the main flaw.
  • The United States sits second. Brilliant for two centuries, now the most endangered of the three. V-Dem recorded the steepest one-year US decline on record in 2025. The 2026 midterms were the voters pushing back.
  • Indonesia's design is the worst of the three. Fixed presidential terms plus a fragmented parliament produce oversized coalitions and no real opposition. V-Dem classed Indonesia an "electoral autocracy" in 2024.
  • Elections are the one check that still works everywhere. Malaysia 2018 and 2022, Indonesia 2024, the US 2026. In three countries the ballot still bites.

Why did Malaysia need four prime ministers in four years? Why did America's elected president nearly stay in office after losing a vote? Why did Indonesia's biggest protests since Reformasi erupt in 2025 over, of all things, housing allowances for parliamentarians?

Three different continents, three different newsroom beats. But once you line the three stories up, the pattern becomes obvious. Each country is a democracy. Each one has been running into the limits of the particular democratic design it inherited. And the differences in those designs are not cosmetic — they decide what kind of crisis each country keeps having, and how quickly, if at all, it can recover.

I spent a week reading constitutional law papers, democracy indices and news reports on all three countries. This piece is what came out. It is long because the question is. If you are pressed for time, the summary box above is the whole argument in miniature. The rest is the working.

01

Why each design took its shape

Constitutions come out of specific fears. Each of these three was written to escape something that the generation writing it had just lived through.

United States: a constitution designed to stop power concentrating

The 1787 Constitution created a presidential, federal republic. The president is elected separately from Congress for a fixed four-year term. The House is apportioned by population; the Senate gives two seats per state, regardless of size. Federal judges serve for life. Judicial review — the Supreme Court's power to strike down laws as unconstitutional — was established by the Court itself in Marbury v. Madison (1803), not written into the text.

The framers were simultaneously afraid of two things. They had just escaped a king, so they feared executive overreach. But they had also lived under the Articles of Confederation (1781-89), which had been too weak to collect taxes or raise armies, so they feared ineffectual government. Their solution, articulated in James Madison's Federalist No. 51, was to make "ambition counteract ambition" — build three branches that would check each other because each would defend its own power. The aim was not efficiency. It was to stop any faction or individual from controlling the whole state.

Bargains with slave states built minority vetoes into the design from the start — the three-fifths clause, equal Senate representation regardless of population, the Electoral College. These compromises gave disproportionate power to smaller, more rural states. They have never been removed.

Amendment is near-impossible. Two-thirds of both houses of Congress and three-quarters of the states must agree. Only 17 amendments have been added since 1791. The US therefore reforms by statute, by regulation and by Supreme Court interpretation, but almost never by amending the constitution itself. The design has to absorb the full stress of every new century, including this highly polarised one, without the text changing.

Malaysia: a negotiated Westminster monarchy

The 1957 Federal Constitution created a federal parliamentary constitutional monarchy. The Yang di-Pertuan Agong is elected every five years from among nine hereditary Malay rulers. The prime minister must command the confidence of the elected Dewan Rakyat. The Dewan Negara is largely appointed and weak. Elections use first-past-the-post in single-member constituencies. Article 153 protects the "special position" of Malays and the natives of Sabah and Sarawak, while guaranteeing citizenship to non-Malays who met independence-era criteria.

Independence was negotiated by an inter-communal elite coalition — UMNO, MCA and MIC, later consolidated as Barisan Nasional. The constitution codified that elite power-sharing. Political scientists call it "consociational": ethnic division is managed by having each community's elites cooperate inside a dominant coalition. For 61 years, until 2018, Malaysia was governed by a single coalition descended from that arrangement.

The design has evolved in response to crises. After the 13 May 1969 riots, parliament was suspended until 1971 and the New Economic Policy launched. Under Mahathir (1981-2003), constitutional amendments curbed the royal veto and subordinated the judiciary. The 2018 election delivered the first turnover of power since independence to Pakatan Harapan. Undi18 (in force 2021) and the anti-party-hopping law (Article 49A, 2022) came later, each reacting to a specific failure.

Indonesia: a revolutionary constitution, redesigned by negotiation

Indonesia's 1945 Constitution created a dominant president chosen by the People's Consultative Assembly. The 1950s experiment with parliamentary government produced 17 cabinets in nine years, which Sukarno used to justify "Guided Democracy" in 1959 and a return to the strong presidency.

The 1965-66 period was catastrophic. An alleged coup attempt was followed by a military-led massacre of perhaps 500,000 to one million suspected communists and ethnic Chinese Indonesians. General Suharto took power in 1966 and ruled for 32 years as the "New Order", built on the military's dwifungsi doctrine — a permanent armed-forces role in both defence and civilian administration.

The Asian financial crisis brought him down in May 1998. Between 1999 and 2002 the MPR amended the 1945 Constitution four times. Presidents were limited to two terms and directly elected with run-offs (first held 2004). A Constitutional Court was established (2003). Impeachment was made conditional on a Constitutional Court legal finding. A weak Regional Representative Council (DPD) was added. The decentralisation laws of 1999 and 2001 moved significant resources to the districts. The Corruption Eradication Commission (KPK) was created with strong investigative and prosecutorial powers.

Three things about this transition matter. It was consensus-based, negotiated inside an MPR that included New Order holdovers. The strong presidency survived because the 1950s parliamentary period was remembered as chaotic. And the DPD was deliberately kept weak by DPR party elites, who did not want a rival chamber.

03

How each system is failing right now

Three recent crises, one per country. These are not ancient history — they happened while we were all watching.

Malaysia · 2020-2022 Recovered

The Sheraton Move and the anti-hopping fix

On 23 February 2020, a group of MPs led by Azmin Ali walked out of the elected Pakatan Harapan coalition at a meeting in a Petaling Jaya hotel. Mahathir resigned the next day. Over the next 32 months the country had four prime ministers. Parliament was suspended for seven months in 2021 under emergency rule.

Three things saved the system. The Malay Rulers refused Muhyiddin's first emergency request in October 2020. UMNO withdrew its support in August 2021, forcing his resignation within days. Ismail Sabri, needing opposition support to pass a budget, signed a confidence-and-supply memorandum with Pakatan Harapan — trading reforms for stability.

The outcome was the Anti-Party-Hopping law (Constitution Amendment No. 3 Act 2022), passed unanimously on 28 July 2022. Article 49A requires an MP who resigns from or ceases to be a member of their party to vacate the seat. The November 2022 election produced Malaysia's first hung parliament; the Agong brokered a unity government under Anwar Ibrahim combining former arch-enemies. Najib Razak's 1MDB conviction followed in December 2025.

United States · 2021-2026 Correction pending

January 6 to the 2026 midterms

On 6 January 2021, a crowd summoned by the sitting president stormed the Capitol to stop certification of the electoral college result. Five died. The system held in the short term because individual officials — many of them Republicans — refused to overturn the vote. State officials certified results; Vice-President Pence declined to reject electoral votes; a 2022 review by eight conservative lawyers found Trump's post-election lawsuits lost 63 of 64.

Accountability then ran into the design's limits. The Senate impeachment vote on 13 February 2021 was 57-43, ten short of conviction. In Trump v. United States (1 July 2024, 6-3), the Supreme Court granted former presidents broad immunity for official acts. In 2025, V-Dem recorded the steepest one-year US democracy decline on record — the Liberal Democracy Index falling from 0.75 to 0.57, with legislative constraints on the executive losing a third of their value in twelve months.

The 3 November 2026 midterms flipped both chambers to the Democrats, with Senate seats changing hands in Kansas, Iowa, Texas, Ohio, Maine and the Carolinas. Northeastern University cited "voter dissatisfaction over the Trump-initiated U.S. war with Iran, rising gas prices and broader economic anxieties." Whether the correction holds depends on what Congress does with its majorities from January 2027.

Indonesia · 2019-2025 Deteriorating

The quiet capture

In September 2019, the DPR passed Law 19/2019, moving the KPK under the executive branch and requiring supervisory-board approval for wiretaps and searches. Mass student protests under the #ReformasiDikorupsi banner did not stop it. Indonesia's CPI score fell from 40 (2019) to 34 (2025).

In October 2023, Constitutional Court Decision 90/PUU-XXI/2023 created an exception to the minimum age rule for presidential and vice-presidential candidates, opening the vice-presidential race to Jokowi's 36-year-old son Gibran. The Court's Honorary Council subsequently found Chief Justice Anwar Usman — Gibran's uncle — guilty of a serious ethics violation and removed him as chief, but ruled it could not reverse the decision. Gibran became Vice-President under Prabowo in October 2024.

In March 2025, the DPR revised the TNI Law to expand civilian positions open to active military officers from 10 to 14 and extend retirement ages. In August 2025, protests over DPR housing allowances (Rp50 million per month on top of total earnings exceeding Rp230 million) turned deadly after a police vehicle killed motorcycle-taxi driver Affan Kurniawan on 28 August. V-Dem classified Indonesia as an "electoral autocracy" in 2024 and maintained that classification in 2025.

05

How each design handles a government that goes bad

A government going bad is the test every democracy eventually faces. The design decides what happens next.

06

Who actually holds power

Constitutions describe formal institutions. Real power often flows through channels the text does not name.

In the United States, after Citizens United v. FEC (2010) corporations, unions and super PACs can spend unlimited independent money on elections. Low-turnout primaries reward activist candidates and the wealthiest campaigns. Gilens and Page (2014), studying 1,779 policy issues, found that economic elites and business groups had substantial independent influence on outcomes while average citizens had little. V-Dem's 2025 assessment is more worrying still: formal separation of powers can be emptied out when one party controls both political branches and its legislators defer to the leader.

In Malaysia, the party-GLC complex has long been the central informal structure. Edmund Terence Gomez and Jomo K.S. documented how UMNO's dominance was financed through rents shared via party channels. The Malay Rulers hold reserve powers that have been decisive — the Agong appointed Muhyiddin after personally interviewing MPs in February 2020; a special meeting of the Rulers rejected his emergency request in October 2020; the Agong brokered Anwar's appointment after the 2022 hung parliament; and the Pardons Board, chaired by the Agong, has reshaped Najib's sentence. Borneo kingmakers (GPS in Sarawak with 23 seats, GRS in Sabah) have become essential to Anwar's majority.

In Indonesia, Jeffrey Winters (Oligarchy, 2011) and Robison and Hadiz argue that New Order oligarchs adapted to democracy by acquiring parties and media. Several large parties are substantially controlled by their chairs: Megawati at PDI-P, Surya Paloh at NasDem, Prabowo at Gerindra before his election as president. The 2025 TNI law revision continues the expansion of civilian roles open to active military officers. The 2023 dynastic manoeuvring through the Constitutional Court showed the judiciary can be used to engineer outcomes. In all three measures — oligarchic, military and dynastic — Indonesia's informal power is more concentrated and less contested than Malaysia's or America's.

Watch for this pattern

In all three countries, small groups can shape the long-term direction despite elections. Indonesia's concentration is the most consequential because its formal counterweights — the KPK, opposition parties, the courts — are the weakest.

08

Is one system actually better?

The following is my own analysis, not findings from the sources. The sources generally take a more cautious "it depends on institutions" line. I think that line is half-true and half-complacent.

Design is not everything. Institutions, state capacity, political culture and the distribution of economic power do most of the day-to-day work of making a system function well or badly. But the "it depends" answer stops too soon. Design matters in a specific, important way: it decides what happens when a system fails. That is the question every democracy eventually has to answer, and it is the question on which these three designs differ most clearly.

#1 Malaysia — Westminster parliamentarism, most resilient under stress

Westminster wins on the single most important question a political system has to answer: what happens when a government goes bad? A parliamentary system removes a failing or captured government through a confidence vote. A presidential system makes you wait out a fixed term unless you can clear the impeachment supermajority bar, which in a polarised country you almost never can. That one difference does more to protect a democracy over time than any other design feature.

Malaysia proved it three times over in seven years. In 2018, voters delivered the first electoral turnover after 61 years of one-party rule. In 2020-22, when defections overturned the mandate, the design absorbed the shock: parliament kept meeting, elections happened on schedule, a hung parliament produced a unity government through the Agong's reserve power, and the flaw was patched by constitutional amendment within two years. In 2025, a former prime minister's 1MDB conviction was final while his own party sat in government.

The problem isn't the design. The problem is what's layered on top — race-based parties, patronage networks, defection culture, a political-financing gap, PM-controlled prosecution, opaque royal clemency. Fix those, and the design works. Each can be fixed by ordinary legislation if the political will exists.

#2 United States — separation of powers, brilliant, now endangered

The US design is the most honest about distrust. Separation of powers, federalism, judicial review, a free press written into the First Amendment — these are genuinely powerful protections, and they worked for two centuries. The design is still better than Indonesia's.

But it rests on a condition the framers never had to think about: that the people inside each branch would defend their branch's prerogatives against a president of their own party. The framers lived in an era of loose factions, not disciplined national parties with a fused media apparatus. When disciplined parties took over, the design lost its built-in self-defence. The 0.75 to 0.57 collapse on V-Dem's index in 2025 happened under an unchanged constitution. The constitution didn't fail. The people inside it stopped defending it.

Voters eventually pushed back at the 2026 midterms. Whether the system corrects itself from here depends on what the new congressional majorities actually do — oversight hearings, appropriations leverage, blocking nominations, protecting independent agencies — and whether courts enforce what Congress passes. These are post-2027 questions.

#3 Indonesia — multiparty presidentialism, worst by design

Indonesia's system is the worst of the three, and this is not an Indonesian failure — it is what the design produces. The system fuses the worst feature of presidentialism (fixed terms, no exit) with the worst feature of multiparty parliamentarism (a fragmented legislature with no majority). The only way to govern is to buy off every significant party with cabinet seats, which guarantees that there is no opposition left to oppose anything.

Jokowi did it: by his second term his coalition held roughly 82% of DPR seats. Prabowo is doing the same. The KPK was weakened in 2019 because no party in the coalition had an interest in protecting it. The TNI law expanded in 2025 the same way. The Gibran candidacy exploited the Constitutional Court the same way. The 2024 downgrade to "electoral autocracy" was already written into the design when it was adopted.

If someone handed you a blank slate today and asked you to design a democracy from scratch, nobody serious would recommend multiparty presidentialism. Scholarly debate since Juan Linz's 1990 paper "The Perils of Presidentialism" has been overwhelmingly between parliamentarism and mixed systems (France, Germany). Multiparty presidentialism combines the rigidities of both.

My take

Westminster: removes the government, keeps the state. Fast, legitimate, no constitutional crisis.

US presidential: survives if 67 senators agree to convict. In a polarised era, they don't. The system waits for the electoral cycle.

Indonesian multiparty presidential: has no mechanism at all. The street becomes the opposition.

Constitutional design is the shock absorber of a democracy. You only notice it when you hit a bump. All three countries have been hitting bumps. Only one design has absorbed them.

09

What each country could actually do

Pointing at designs is only half the job. Here is what I would do if I were advising each country, taking the design as a given.

1
Malaysia: fix what's layered on top, not the design itself Pass a comprehensive political-financing law so 1MDB-style capture is not structurally repeatable. Close the anti-hopping-law loophole for expelled MPs. Separate the Attorney General from the Public Prosecutor so the executive doesn't control who gets charged. Make Pardons Board decisions reasoned and public. Measure: visible prosecution of political patrons regardless of coalition membership
2
United States: patch a near-unamendable constitution by statute Build durable federal legislation on election administration, political financing, judicial ethics and congressional oversight. The Electoral Count Reform Act of 2022 is the model — a technical bipartisan fix of a specific vulnerability. Similar patches are available on Supreme Court conflicts of interest, inspector-general independence, and uniform election standards. Measure: V-Dem Liberal Democracy Index stops falling by 2028 and begins recovering
3
Indonesia: shrink the president's leverage over parliament Raise the parliamentary threshold so fewer parties qualify for the DPR, giving the president fewer parties to buy off. Protect the KPK's independence constitutionally so it cannot be weakened by ordinary legislation. Draw a bright line against active military officers in civilian positions, reversing the 2025 TNI law expansion. Measure: DPR opposition bloc restored to meaningful size (above 25% of seats) by 2029

Hard questions for each of us

Design arguments are easy. Living inside one is not. A few questions I keep coming back to.

For MalaysiansIs the anti-hopping law being quietly unwound through party-constitution tricks, and would we even notice?
For AmericansIf a future Senate is never going to convict a president of your own party, what else carries the weight of accountability?
For IndonesiansWould you accept raising the parliamentary threshold if it meant your smaller preferred party disappeared but opposition in parliament survived?
For anyoneIf your country's design is good, who inside it is still defending it today? If no one, is the design really the thing holding?
For voters everywhereAre you voting for the person, or for the institution they would weaken or strengthen? Those are different votes.

What to remember

  • Ask what a system does when a government goes bad. That tells you more than any index does.
  • Westminster parliamentarism removes a failing government in weeks. Presidential systems need a supermajority — and polarisation makes that supermajority near-impossible.
  • Multiparty presidentialism tends toward oversized coalitions and absorbed oppositions. It is the worst combination on offer.
  • A good constitution can perform badly if the people inside it stop defending their own institutions. The US in 2025 is a live demonstration.
  • Elections remain the one check that still works in all three countries. 2018 Malaysia, 2024 Indonesia, 2026 America. The vote still bites.
